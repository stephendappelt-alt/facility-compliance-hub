## The Regulatory Landscape of Generator Fuel Storage

Managing fuel for commercial emergency generators involves far more than keeping the tank full. Fuel storage and management is governed by a layered framework of federal, state, and local regulations that address environmental protection, fire safety, fuel quality, and emergency preparedness. Non-compliance with any layer of this framework can result in significant fines, environmental remediation costs, and operational disruptions.

For facility managers overseeing diesel-powered emergency generators, the primary regulatory authorities include the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for spill prevention and environmental protection, the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) for fire safety standards related to fuel storage and for the generator standard itself ([NFPA 110](/generators/nfpa-110-emergency-generator-compliance-guide)), and ASTM International for fuel quality specifications. State and local regulations add additional requirements that vary by jurisdiction.

This guide covers the essential compliance requirements across all of these frameworks so you can build a fuel management program that meets code, protects the environment, and ensures your generator fuel is ready to perform during an emergency.

## EPA SPCC Plan Requirements

The EPA's Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure (SPCC) rule under 40 CFR Part 112 is the primary federal regulation governing fuel storage at commercial facilities. Its purpose is to prevent oil discharges into navigable waters and adjoining shorelines.

### Who Needs an SPCC Plan

Your facility must prepare and implement an SPCC plan if both of the following conditions are met:

- The facility stores oil (including diesel fuel, lubricating oil, and hydraulic fluid) in quantities that exceed the regulatory threshold
- The facility could reasonably be expected, because of its location, to discharge oil in harmful quantities into navigable waters or adjoining shorelines

### SPCC Storage Thresholds

| Storage Type | Capacity Threshold |
|---|---|
| Aboveground storage (aggregate) | Greater than 1,320 gallons, counting only containers of 55 gallons or more |
| Completely buried storage (aggregate) | Greater than 42,000 gallons, not counting USTs subject to the federal UST rules in 40 CFR Part 280 or an approved state program |

The rule is triggered if either threshold is exceeded. **Aggregate capacity** is based on the capacity of every oil container of 55 gallons or more at the facility, not just generator fuel tanks. This can include diesel day tanks, generator base tanks, bulk storage tanks, drums, oil-filled equipment such as transformers, and other oil stored on-site. The EPA Regional Administrator can also require a plan from a facility that would otherwise be exempt.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistake">
It is easy to overlook smaller containers when calculating aggregate storage. For example, a 1,000-gallon generator base tank, a 200-gallon day tank, and two 55-gallon drums of oil add up to 1,310 gallons -- one more drum or an oil-filled transformer pushes the site past the 1,320-gallon threshold. Count every container of 55 gallons or more.
</Callout>

### SPCC Plan Contents

Under 40 CFR 112.7, an SPCC plan must include, among other things:

- **Facility diagram** marking the location and contents of each fixed oil storage container, transfer stations, and connecting piping
- **Oil storage inventory** listing the type of oil in each fixed container and its storage capacity
- **Discharge prevention measures** for routine handling, including loading and unloading
- **Drainage controls and containment** describing secondary containment around containers (dikes, berms, double-walled tanks)
- **Countermeasures** for discovering, responding to, and cleaning up discharges, plus disposal methods
- **Contact list** including the facility response coordinator and the National Response Center
- **Inspections and tests** with written procedures; records must be signed and kept with the plan for three years
- **Personnel training** covering equipment operation and maintenance, discharge procedures, and applicable pollution control laws, plus discharge prevention briefings for oil-handling personnel at least once a year
- **Professional Engineer certification** -- the plan must be reviewed and certified by a licensed Professional Engineer (PE) unless the facility qualifies to self-certify as a Tier I or Tier II qualified facility

The owner must also review and evaluate the plan at least once every five years.

### Tier I and Tier II Qualified Facilities

Smaller facilities may qualify for simplified SPCC plan requirements:

**Tier II Qualified Facility:**
- Aggregate aboveground storage capacity of 10,000 gallons or less
- In the three years before self-certifying, no single discharge over 1,000 gallons and no two discharges each over 42 gallons within any 12-month period (discharges from natural disasters, acts of war, or terrorism excluded)
- The owner may self-certify the plan; a PE must still review and certify any environmentally equivalent alternative or any impracticability determination for secondary containment

**Tier I Qualified Facility:**
- Meets the Tier II criteria, and
- Has no individual aboveground container larger than 5,000 gallons
- May complete and self-certify the EPA template in Appendix G to Part 112 instead of a full plan

Facilities that do not meet these criteria need a full SPCC plan certified by a PE.

### Secondary Containment Requirements

For bulk storage containers, the SPCC rule (40 CFR 112.8(c)(2)) requires secondary containment for the entire capacity of the largest single container plus sufficient freeboard to contain precipitation. The federal rule does not set a fixed percentage for that freeboard, so size it for your site's rainfall and check whether state or local codes set a specific capacity. For generator fuel tanks, secondary containment typically means:

- **Double-walled tanks** -- A common solution for generator fuel storage, where the outer wall serves as the secondary containment
- **Containment berms or dikes** -- Constructed around single-walled tanks, sized for the largest tank plus precipitation freeboard
- **Containment pallets** -- For smaller containers such as drums and totes

Aboveground containers must be tested or inspected for integrity on a regular schedule, with the frequency and inspector qualifications set by industry standards. Rainwater may not be drained from a diked area to a storm drain or open watercourse unless the bypass valve is normally sealed closed, the water is inspected to confirm it will not cause a harmful discharge, and each drainage event is recorded.

## NFPA 30 and NFPA 37 Fuel Storage Requirements

While the EPA addresses environmental protection, NFPA standards address the fire safety aspects of fuel storage.

### NFPA 30: Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code

NFPA 30 provides requirements for the storage, handling, and use of flammable and combustible liquids, including diesel fuel. NFPA 30 defines a Class II liquid as one with a flash point at or above 100 degrees F and below 140 degrees F. No. 2 diesel's minimum flash point under ASTM D975 is 52 degrees C (about 126 degrees F), which falls in that range.

Key requirements under NFPA 30 for generator fuel storage include:

- **Tank construction standards** -- Atmospheric aboveground tanks must be designed and built to one of several recognized standards, one of which is UL 142 (steel aboveground tanks); UL 142 listings also cover special-purpose tanks such as generator base tanks and day tanks
- **Separation distances** -- Minimum distances between fuel tanks and buildings, property lines, and other tanks based on tank capacity and construction type
- **Venting** -- Tanks must have emergency and normal venting provisions to prevent overpressure during filling and thermal expansion
- **Spill control** -- Provisions to prevent and contain spills during fuel delivery operations
- **Fire protection** -- Requirements for fire suppression or protection based on tank location and size

### NFPA 37: Standard for the Installation and Use of Stationary Combustion Engines and Gas Turbines

NFPA 37 addresses fuel supply requirements specific to stationary engines, including emergency generators:

- **Fuel supply piping** -- Requirements for fuel line materials, routing, and protection
- **Day tank provisions** -- Sizing and overflow protection for integral and remote day tanks
- **Fuel system components** -- Requirements for fuel filters, valves, flexible connections, and leak detection
- **Indoor fuel storage** -- Limits on the size of fuel tanks inside buildings, with larger quantities permitted only in rooms that meet added construction or fire protection requirements

<Callout type="info" title="Indoor Fuel Storage Limits">
Under NFPA 37 (2015 edition), fuel tanks inside buildings may not exceed 660 gallons when located in a shared space. Larger tanks are allowed in dedicated rooms that meet added construction requirements, and an exception permits tanks of any size in engine or mechanical rooms designed with suitable fire detection, suppression, and containment. That exception is open to interpretation, so coordinate early with your AHJ, and check local fire code rules, which may be stricter. See our guide to [generator room design](/generators/generator-room-design-nfpa-37-ventilation-clearances).
</Callout>

## Fuel Quality Testing and ASTM D975 Standards

Stored diesel fuel degrades over time. Normally produced diesel has adequate stability for ordinary storage and use, but ASTM D975 treats storage beyond 12 months as long-term storage and advises a plan for monitoring fuel quality during prolonged storage.

NFPA 110 builds fuel quality into generator compliance. It requires tanks to be sized so the fuel is used within its storage life, or else provisions to remediate or replace stale or contaminated fuel, and it requires a fuel quality test at least annually using appropriate ASTM standards or the manufacturer's recommendations (Section 8.3.7 in the 2022 and 2025 editions; Section 8.3.8 in the 2010 edition).

### ASTM D975: Standard Specification for Diesel Fuel

ASTM D975 is the primary specification for diesel fuel quality in the United States. The limits below are for the No. 2-D grades in Table 1 of the 2024 edition (D975-24); check the current edition when you set up a testing contract.

| Property | ASTM D975 Requirement | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Cetane number | 40 minimum | Affects cold starting and combustion quality |
| Water and sediment | 0.05% maximum by volume | Water promotes microbial growth and corrosion |
| Sulfur content | Varies by grade (15 ppm maximum for No. 2-D S15, ultra-low sulfur diesel) | Emissions compliance and engine compatibility |
| Flash point | 52 degrees C minimum (about 126 degrees F) | Fire safety during storage and handling |
| Kinematic viscosity (at 40 degrees C) | 1.9 to 4.1 mm2/s | Affects fuel injection and atomization |
| Ash | 0.01% maximum by mass | Indicates contamination or additive residue |
| Ramsbottom carbon residue (on 10% distillation residue) | 0.35% maximum by mass | Indicates tendency to form deposits |

### Common Fuel Degradation Issues

**Microbial contamination** -- Microbial growth (often called "diesel bug") can occur at the fuel-water interface in storage tanks. The resulting biomass and byproducts can clog filters and contribute to tank corrosion. ASTM D6469 is the guide ASTM D975 points to for this form of contamination.

**Oxidative degradation** -- Over time, fuel can form soluble gums and insoluble degradation products that lead to deposits and filter plugging. ASTM D975 notes that this degradation can be catalyzed by dissolved metals, especially copper and zinc.

**Water accumulation** -- Water enters tanks through condensation, fill caps, vents, and degraded seals. ASTM D975 notes that water promotes corrosion and microbial growth and recommends provisions for draining water on a scheduled basis.

**Heat** -- High storage temperatures may accelerate fuel degradation. ASTM D975 suggests sheltering aboveground tanks or painting them with reflective paint, and keeping fixed-roof tanks full to limit oxygen and tank breathing.

### Recommended Fuel Testing Schedule

NFPA 110 itself requires only the annual fuel quality test. The other intervals below are common industry practice; set yours based on fuel turnover, tank conditions, and manufacturer guidance.

| Test | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Visual inspection (clarity and color) | Monthly | Quick check for obvious contamination |
| Water bottom check | Monthly | Detect water accumulation for draining |
| Microbial contamination test | Periodically (for example, semi-annually) or when water is found | Detect bacterial and fungal growth |
| Fuel quality test per ASTM standards | At least annually (NFPA 110 requirement) | Comprehensive quality verification |
| Post-delivery verification | Each delivery | Confirm new fuel meets specifications |

<Callout type="tip" title="Fuel Sampling Best Practice">
Contaminants and degradation products usually settle to the bottom of a still tank, so a sample drawn from higher up can look clean while problems sit below. ASTM D975 recommends including a bottom (or clearance) sample along with an all-level sample, following ASTM D4057 sampling practice. Use a bottom-sampling valve or a sampling thief designed for tank bottom access.
</Callout>

## Fuel Polishing and Maintenance

Fuel polishing is the process of circulating stored fuel through a filtration and water separation system to remove particulates, water, and microbial contamination. It is a common way to remediate fuel in long-term storage, which is one of the options NFPA 110 allows when fuel is not used within its storage life (the other is replacing it with fresh fuel).

### When Fuel Polishing Is Needed

- Fuel has been in long-term storage (more than 12 months) without testing or treatment
- Microbial testing indicates contamination
- Filter plugging has been observed during generator operation or testing
- Water accumulation has been detected at the tank bottom
- The annual fuel quality test indicates degradation in any parameter

### Fuel Polishing Methods

**Portable fuel polishing systems** -- A contractor brings a mobile filtration unit to the facility, connects it to the tank, and circulates the fuel through multi-stage filtration. This is a common approach for facilities that polish periodically rather than continuously.

**Permanently installed systems** -- Larger facilities or those with critical uptime requirements may install fixed fuel polishing systems that operate on a timer or continuous circulation basis. These systems typically include particulate filters, water separators, and fuel conditioning additives.

**Fuel additive treatment** -- Biocides and stability additives can supplement mechanical polishing but should not be considered a standalone solution. Biocides kill microbial contamination but do not remove the biomass from the fuel; filtration is still required to remove the dead organisms and their byproducts.

### Recommended Fuel Maintenance Schedule

These intervals are typical practice, not code requirements, except where noted.

| Activity | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Visual tank inspection | Monthly | Check for leaks, corrosion, and containment integrity |
| Water bottom drain | Monthly or as detected | Remove all free water from tank bottom |
| Fuel polishing | As indicated by fuel testing (many sites polish annually) | More frequently if contamination is detected |
| Biocide treatment | As needed per microbial test results | Follow manufacturer dosing instructions |
| Filter replacement | Per manufacturer schedule or differential pressure | Do not extend beyond recommended intervals |
| Tank cleaning | As indicated by inspections and fuel testing | Full internal cleaning and inspection |

## Tank Inspection Requirements: STI SP001

The SPCC rule requires aboveground containers to be inspected or tested on a schedule set by industry standards. For shop-fabricated tanks like most generator fuel tanks, the standard most often used is the Steel Tank Institute's SP001, Standard for the Inspection of Aboveground Storage Tanks.

### STI SP001 Inspection Categories

**Periodic inspections** -- Monthly and annual inspections performed by the tank owner or operator (someone knowledgeable in tank operations) to check the tank, piping, equipment, secondary containment, and foundation for leaks, corrosion, and damage. Document them on the SP001 checklists.

**Formal inspections and leak tests** -- Formal external and internal inspections performed by a certified inspector, plus leak tests where required. Whether a tank needs them, and how often, depends on the tank's size, construction, and risk category, which is based on whether it has spill control and a continuous release detection method. Use the SP001 inspection schedule table for your specific tank rather than a single rule-of-thumb interval.

**Formal inspection elements include:**
- External visual examination of all tank surfaces, supports, and foundations
- Assessment of coating condition and corrosion
- Evaluation of fittings, valves, and piping connections
- Verification of emergency venting capability
- Secondary containment integrity assessment
- Ultrasonic thickness testing of tank walls and bottom (if accessible)
- Internal inspection when warranted by external findings

## Above-Ground vs. Underground Storage Considerations

The choice between above-ground storage tanks (ASTs) and underground storage tanks (USTs) significantly affects the regulatory requirements applicable to your facility.

### Above-Ground Storage Tanks

**Regulatory framework:** EPA SPCC, NFPA 30, state fire codes, local zoning

**Advantages:**
- Easier to inspect and maintain visually
- Leak detection is straightforward (visible leaks)
- Lower installation cost than underground tanks
- Easier to add secondary containment (double-walled construction)

**Disadvantages:**
- Subject to weather exposure and temperature extremes
- Requires dedicated outdoor space with separation distances
- More vulnerable to physical damage from vehicles and equipment
- Visual impact on property aesthetics

### Underground Storage Tanks

**Regulatory framework:** EPA UST regulations (40 CFR Parts 280-282), state UST programs, NFPA 30

**Advantages:**
- Space-efficient; no above-ground footprint
- Protected from weather and physical damage
- Reduced fire exposure risk

**Disadvantages:**
- Subject to extensive EPA UST regulations including leak detection, financial responsibility, and closure requirements
- Leaks are difficult to detect without monitoring equipment
- Installation and removal costs are substantially higher
- Environmental remediation costs for leaking USTs can be extreme
- Many states require registration, permits, and fees

<Callout type="danger" title="UST Regulatory Warning">
Underground fuel storage tanks are subject to comprehensive federal and state UST regulations that go well beyond SPCC requirements. These include mandatory leak detection systems, operator training, financial responsibility demonstration, and eventual closure and removal requirements. The regulatory burden for USTs is significantly greater than for above-ground storage. Consult your state UST program before installing underground fuel storage.
</Callout>

## State-Specific Regulatory Overlays

Federal regulations establish a baseline, and state and local rules can add to it. Requirements vary widely, so treat the following as a checklist of questions to ask rather than a list of rules that apply everywhere:

- **State tank programs** -- Does your state have its own aboveground storage tank or spill-prevention program, and at what capacity does it apply?
- **Registration requirements** -- Must aboveground fuel tanks be registered with the state environmental agency?
- **Containment standards** -- Does the state or local fire code set a specific secondary containment capacity?
- **Air quality permits** -- Does your generator need a state air permit that limits operating hours or fuel sulfur content? Federal rules also limit emergency engines to 100 hours per year of maintenance and readiness testing (see our guide to [EPA emissions standards for backup generators](/generators/epa-emissions-standards-commercial-backup-generators)).
- **Local fire department permits** -- Does your fire department require a permit and inspections for fuel storage above a certain quantity?

Always check with your state environmental agency, state fire marshal's office, and local fire department to identify requirements that may apply in addition to federal standards.

## Documentation Requirements

Comprehensive documentation is essential across all fuel management regulatory frameworks. Your fuel management records should include:

### SPCC Documentation
- Current SPCC plan with PE certification or self-certification, as applicable
- Signed records of inspections and tests, kept with the plan for three years
- Records of rainwater drainage from diked areas
- Spill event reports and response documentation
- Personnel training and annual discharge prevention briefing records
- Documentation of the five-year plan review and any amendments

### Fuel Quality Documentation
- Fuel delivery receipts with supplier, quantity, date, and grade
- All fuel testing results, including the annual fuel quality test required by NFPA 110
- Fuel polishing service records with dates, volumes processed, and contamination findings
- Biocide treatment records with product, dosage, and application date

### Tank Inspection Documentation
- Monthly routine inspection checklists
- Formal inspection reports per STI SP001 or applicable standard
- Repair and maintenance records
- Cathodic protection system testing (if applicable)

### Operational Documentation
- Fuel inventory records (deliveries, consumption, current level)
- Tank gauging records
- Fuel system maintenance records (filters, valves, piping)
- Emergency response plan and contact information

<Callout type="tip" title="Documentation Best Practice">
Create a single fuel management binder or digital file that consolidates all fuel-related documentation. Organize it by regulatory program (SPCC, NFPA, fuel quality) with a master index. This simplifies both internal audits and regulatory inspections.
</Callout>

## Building a Compliant Fuel Management Program

An effective fuel management program integrates all of the regulatory requirements discussed above into a cohesive operational framework:

1. **Calculate your aggregate oil storage capacity** and determine whether the SPCC rule applies to your facility
2. **Develop or update your SPCC plan** with PE certification if required
3. **Verify your tank construction and installation** meets NFPA 30 and local fire code requirements
4. **Implement a monthly inspection program** covering visual checks, water bottom draining, and containment integrity
5. **Establish a fuel testing schedule** that includes at least an annual fuel quality test using ASTM standards, plus more frequent checks based on your fuel turnover and conditions
6. **Plan for fuel remediation** -- periodic polishing, a permanent polishing system for large fuel inventories, or scheduled fuel replacement
7. **Schedule tank inspections** per STI SP001 or another applicable industry standard, based on your tank's size, construction, and category
8. **Train all relevant personnel** on spill prevention, fuel handling, and emergency response procedures
9. **Maintain consolidated documentation** that is current, accessible, and audit-ready
10. **Review your program annually** to reflect regulatory changes, equipment modifications, and operational experience, and complete the formal SPCC plan review at least every five years

Fuel management compliance is not a one-time effort. It requires ongoing attention, regular testing, and systematic documentation. But the investment pays dividends in generator reliability, environmental protection, and regulatory peace of mind.
