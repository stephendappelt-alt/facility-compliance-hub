## The Regulatory Landscape of Generator Fuel Storage

Managing fuel for commercial emergency generators involves far more than keeping the tank full. Fuel storage and management is governed by a layered framework of federal, state, and local regulations that address environmental protection, fire safety, fuel quality, and emergency preparedness. Non-compliance with any layer of this framework can result in significant fines, environmental remediation costs, and operational disruptions.

For facility managers overseeing diesel-powered emergency generators, the primary regulatory authorities include the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for spill prevention and environmental protection, the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) for fire safety standards related to fuel storage, and the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) for fuel quality specifications. State and local regulations add additional requirements that vary by jurisdiction.

This guide covers the essential compliance requirements across all of these frameworks so you can build a fuel management program that meets code, protects the environment, and ensures your generator fuel is ready to perform during an emergency.

## EPA SPCC Plan Requirements

The EPA's Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure (SPCC) rule under 40 CFR Part 112 is the primary federal regulation governing fuel storage at commercial facilities. Its purpose is to prevent oil discharges into navigable waters and adjoining shorelines.

### Who Needs an SPCC Plan

Your facility must prepare and implement an SPCC plan if all of the following conditions are met:

- The facility stores oil (including diesel fuel, lubricating oil, and hydraulic fluid) in quantities that exceed the regulatory threshold
- The facility could reasonably be expected to discharge oil into navigable waters or adjoining shorelines

### SPCC Storage Thresholds

| Storage Type | Capacity Threshold |
|---|---|
| Above-ground storage (aggregate) | Greater than 1,320 gallons total |
| Underground storage (aggregate) | Greater than 42,000 gallons total |
| Single above-ground container | Greater than 660 gallons |

**Aggregate capacity** means the combined total of all oil storage containers at the facility, not just generator fuel tanks. This includes diesel day tanks, bulk storage tanks, lubricating oil reservoirs, hydraulic systems, transformer oil, and any other petroleum-based fluid stored on-site.

<Callout type="warning" title="Common Mistake">
Many facility managers overlook smaller containers when calculating aggregate storage. A 500-gallon generator belly tank, a 200-gallon day tank, a 55-gallon drum of hydraulic fluid, and several small containers of lubricating oil can easily push a facility past the 1,320-gallon threshold. Count every container.
</Callout>

### SPCC Plan Contents

A compliant SPCC plan must include the following elements:

- **Facility diagram** showing the location of all oil storage containers, transfer areas, and drainage pathways
- **Oil storage inventory** listing every container with its capacity, product stored, and type of containment
- **Spill history** documenting any spills or discharges within the previous 12 months
- **Containment provisions** describing secondary containment for each storage area (dikes, berms, double-walled tanks)
- **Spill response procedures** detailing immediate response actions, notification requirements, and cleanup methods
- **Inspection schedule** specifying the frequency and scope of inspections for all storage containers and containment systems
- **Personnel training** documenting the training program for employees who handle oil or respond to spills
- **Professional Engineer certification** -- the plan must be certified by a licensed Professional Engineer (PE) unless the facility qualifies for the Tier I or Tier II self-certification provisions

### Tier I and Tier II Qualified Facilities

Smaller facilities may qualify for simplified SPCC plan requirements:

**Tier I Qualified Facility:**
- Aggregate above-ground storage capacity of 10,000 gallons or less
- No single container exceeding 5,000 gallons
- No reportable oil discharge history in the past three years
- May use the EPA's SPCC Plan template and self-certify (no PE required)

**Tier II Qualified Facility:**
- Same capacity thresholds as Tier I
- Requires a PE-certified plan but may use simplified procedures

Facilities that exceed these thresholds require a full SPCC plan certified by a PE.

### Secondary Containment Requirements

The SPCC rule requires secondary containment capable of holding the entire volume of the largest single container plus sufficient freeboard for precipitation. For generator fuel tanks, this typically means:

- **Double-walled tanks** -- The most common solution for generator fuel storage, where the outer wall serves as the secondary containment
- **Containment berms or dikes** -- Constructed around single-walled tanks, sized to hold 110% of the largest tank's volume
- **Containment pallets** -- For smaller containers such as drums and totes

Secondary containment systems must be inspected regularly for integrity, and any accumulated rainwater must be managed through a controlled drainage process -- not simply pumped out without inspection for oil contamination.

## NFPA 30 and NFPA 37 Fuel Storage Requirements

While the EPA addresses environmental protection, NFPA standards address the fire safety aspects of fuel storage.

### NFPA 30: Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code

NFPA 30 provides requirements for the storage, handling, and use of flammable and combustible liquids, including diesel fuel (classified as a Class II combustible liquid with a flash point between 100 and 140 degrees Fahrenheit).

Key requirements under NFPA 30 for generator fuel storage include:

- **Tank construction standards** -- Above-ground tanks must be listed and constructed in accordance with UL 142 (steel above-ground tanks) or equivalent standards
- **Separation distances** -- Minimum distances between fuel tanks and buildings, property lines, and other tanks based on tank capacity and construction type
- **Venting** -- Tanks must have emergency and normal venting provisions to prevent overpressure during filling and thermal expansion
- **Spill control** -- Provisions to prevent and contain spills during fuel delivery operations
- **Fire protection** -- Requirements for fire suppression or protection based on tank location and size

### NFPA 37: Standard for the Installation and Use of Stationary Combustion Engines and Gas Turbines

NFPA 37 addresses fuel supply requirements specific to stationary engines, including emergency generators:

- **Fuel supply piping** -- Requirements for fuel line materials, routing, and protection
- **Day tank provisions** -- Sizing and overflow protection for integral and remote day tanks
- **Fuel system components** -- Requirements for fuel filters, valves, flexible connections, and leak detection
- **Indoor fuel storage** -- Limits on the quantity of fuel that can be stored inside a building (typically limited to the generator's integral fuel tank or a day tank not exceeding specific capacity limits)

<Callout type="info" title="Indoor Fuel Storage Limits">
NFPA 37 limits indoor fuel storage to the fuel contained within the generator's integral base tank plus any day tank approved for indoor use. Check your local AHJ requirements, as some jurisdictions impose stricter limits on indoor diesel storage than the base NFPA standard.
</Callout>

## Fuel Quality Testing and ASTM D975 Standards

Stored diesel fuel degrades over time. Unlike gasoline, which has a relatively short shelf life measured in months, diesel fuel can remain usable for an extended period if properly maintained. However, without active quality management, diesel fuel begins degrading from the day it is delivered.

### ASTM D975: Standard Specification for Diesel Fuel

ASTM D975 is the primary specification for diesel fuel quality in the United States. It establishes minimum requirements for several key fuel properties:

| Property | ASTM D975 Requirement | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Cetane number | 40 minimum | Affects cold starting and combustion quality |
| Water and sediment | 0.05% maximum by volume | Water promotes microbial growth and corrosion |
| Sulfur content | Varies by grade (15 ppm max for ultra-low sulfur) | Emissions compliance and injector wear |
| Flash point | 52 degrees C minimum (125.6 degrees F) | Fire safety during storage and handling |
| Viscosity (at 40 degrees C) | 1.9 to 4.1 mm2/s | Affects fuel injection and atomization |
| Ash content | 0.01% maximum | Indicates contamination or additive residue |
| Carbon residue | 0.35% maximum | Indicates fuel stability and combustion quality |

### Common Fuel Degradation Issues

**Microbial contamination** -- Bacteria, yeasts, and fungi (commonly called "diesel bug") thrive at the fuel-water interface in storage tanks. They produce acidic byproducts that corrode tanks and generate biomass that clogs filters and injectors. Microbial contamination is the most common and destructive form of stored fuel degradation.

**Oxidative degradation** -- Exposure to oxygen causes fuel to form gums, varnishes, and insoluble particles. This process accelerates with temperature and exposure to certain metals (copper and zinc are catalysts). Oxidized fuel darkens in color and produces sediment that clogs filters.

**Water accumulation** -- Water enters tanks through condensation, fill caps, vent systems, and degraded seals. Even a small amount of water promotes microbial growth and can cause injector damage. Water at the bottom of a fuel tank is virtually inevitable over time and must be actively managed.

**Thermal instability** -- Fuel exposed to high temperatures (near engines or in hot climates) degrades faster than fuel stored at stable, moderate temperatures.

### Recommended Fuel Testing Schedule

| Test | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Visual inspection (clarity and color) | Monthly | Quick check for obvious contamination |
| Water bottom check | Monthly | Detect water accumulation for draining |
| Microbial contamination test | Semi-annually | Detect bacterial and fungal growth |
| Full ASTM D975 panel | Annually | Comprehensive quality verification |
| Post-delivery verification | Each delivery | Confirm new fuel meets specifications |

<Callout type="tip" title="Fuel Sampling Best Practice">
Always collect fuel samples from the bottom of the tank, where water and contaminants settle. A sample drawn from the top or middle of the tank may appear clean while significant contamination exists below. Use a bottom-sampling valve or a dedicated sampling thief designed for tank bottom access.
</Callout>

## Fuel Polishing and Maintenance

Fuel polishing is the process of circulating stored fuel through a filtration and water separation system to remove particulates, water, and microbial contamination. It is the primary proactive maintenance strategy for maintaining fuel quality in long-term storage.

### When Fuel Polishing Is Needed

- Fuel has been in storage for more than 12 months without treatment
- Microbial testing indicates contamination
- Filter plugging has been observed during generator operation or testing
- Water accumulation has been detected at the tank bottom
- Annual ASTM testing indicates degradation in any parameter

### Fuel Polishing Methods

**Portable fuel polishing systems** -- A contractor brings a mobile filtration unit to the facility, connects it to the tank, and circulates the fuel through multi-stage filtration. This is the most common approach for facilities with annual fuel polishing needs.

**Permanently installed systems** -- Larger facilities or those with critical uptime requirements may install fixed fuel polishing systems that operate on a timer or continuous circulation basis. These systems typically include particulate filters, water separators, and fuel conditioning additives.

**Fuel additive treatment** -- Biocides and stability additives can supplement mechanical polishing but should not be considered a standalone solution. Biocides kill microbial contamination but do not remove the biomass from the fuel; filtration is still required to remove the dead organisms and their byproducts.

### Recommended Fuel Maintenance Schedule

| Activity | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Visual tank inspection | Monthly | Check for leaks, corrosion, and containment integrity |
| Water bottom drain | Monthly or as detected | Remove all free water from tank bottom |
| Fuel polishing | Annually minimum | More frequently if contamination is detected |
| Biocide treatment | As needed per microbial test results | Follow manufacturer dosing instructions |
| Filter replacement | Per manufacturer schedule or differential pressure | Do not extend beyond recommended intervals |
| Tank cleaning | Every 3-5 years | Full internal cleaning and inspection |

## Tank Inspection Requirements: STI SP001

The Steel Tank Institute's SP001 standard, the Standard for the Inspection of Aboveground Storage Tanks, provides the inspection framework most commonly referenced for generator fuel tanks.

### STI SP001 Inspection Categories

**Routine inspections** -- Monthly visual inspections performed by facility personnel to check for leaks, corrosion, structural damage, and containment integrity. These inspections should be documented on a standard checklist.

**Formal inspections** -- Detailed inspections performed by qualified inspectors (certified per STI SP001 or API 653) at intervals determined by tank age, condition, and contents. For fuel storage tanks in good condition, a formal inspection is typically required every 10 years, with more frequent inspections for older or damaged tanks.

**Formal inspection elements include:**
- External visual examination of all tank surfaces, supports, and foundations
- Assessment of coating condition and corrosion
- Evaluation of fittings, valves, and piping connections
- Verification of emergency venting capability
- Secondary containment integrity assessment
- Ultrasonic thickness testing of tank walls and bottom (if accessible)
- Internal inspection when warranted by external findings

## Above-Ground vs. Underground Storage Considerations

The choice between above-ground storage tanks (ASTs) and underground storage tanks (USTs) significantly affects the regulatory requirements applicable to your facility.

### Above-Ground Storage Tanks

**Regulatory framework:** EPA SPCC, NFPA 30, state fire codes, local zoning

**Advantages:**
- Easier to inspect and maintain visually
- Leak detection is straightforward (visible leaks)
- Lower installation cost than underground tanks
- Easier to add secondary containment (double-walled construction)

**Disadvantages:**
- Subject to weather exposure and temperature extremes
- Requires dedicated outdoor space with separation distances
- More vulnerable to physical damage from vehicles and equipment
- Visual impact on property aesthetics

### Underground Storage Tanks

**Regulatory framework:** EPA UST regulations (40 CFR Parts 280-282), state UST programs, NFPA 30

**Advantages:**
- Space-efficient; no above-ground footprint
- Protected from weather and physical damage
- Reduced fire exposure risk

**Disadvantages:**
- Subject to extensive EPA UST regulations including leak detection, financial responsibility, and closure requirements
- Leaks are difficult to detect without monitoring equipment
- Installation and removal costs are substantially higher
- Environmental remediation costs for leaking USTs can be extreme
- Many states require registration, permits, and annual compliance fees

<Callout type="danger" title="UST Regulatory Warning">
Underground fuel storage tanks are subject to comprehensive federal and state UST regulations that go well beyond SPCC requirements. These include mandatory leak detection systems, operator training, financial responsibility demonstration, and eventual closure and removal requirements. The regulatory burden for USTs is significantly greater than for above-ground storage. Consult your state UST program before installing underground fuel storage.
</Callout>

## State-Specific Regulatory Overlays

Federal regulations establish the minimum compliance baseline, but many states impose additional requirements that exceed federal standards. Common state-level additions include:

- **Lower SPCC thresholds** -- Some states require spill prevention plans at storage capacities below the federal 1,320-gallon threshold
- **Registration requirements** -- Certain states require registration of above-ground fuel storage tanks with the state environmental agency
- **Stricter containment standards** -- Some states require containment capacity exceeding the federal 110% standard
- **Air quality permits** -- Emergency generators above certain horsepower thresholds may require air permits that restrict operating hours and fuel sulfur content
- **Fuel delivery notification** -- Some jurisdictions require advance notification of bulk fuel deliveries
- **Local fire department permits** -- Many municipalities require fire department permits for fuel storage above certain thresholds, with annual inspections

Always check with your state environmental agency, state fire marshal's office, and local fire department to identify requirements that may apply in addition to federal standards.

## Documentation Requirements

Comprehensive documentation is essential across all fuel management regulatory frameworks. Your fuel management records should include:

### SPCC Documentation
- Current SPCC plan with PE certification (if applicable)
- Monthly inspection records for all storage containers and containment
- Spill event reports and response documentation
- Personnel training records with dates and content covered
- Annual plan review documentation

### Fuel Quality Documentation
- Fuel delivery receipts with supplier, quantity, date, and grade
- All fuel testing results (monthly visual, semi-annual microbial, annual ASTM)
- Fuel polishing service records with dates, volumes processed, and contamination findings
- Biocide treatment records with product, dosage, and application date

### Tank Inspection Documentation
- Monthly routine inspection checklists
- Formal inspection reports per STI SP001 or applicable standard
- Repair and maintenance records
- Cathodic protection system testing (if applicable)

### Operational Documentation
- Fuel inventory records (deliveries, consumption, current level)
- Tank gauging records
- Fuel system maintenance records (filters, valves, piping)
- Emergency response plan and contact information

<Callout type="tip" title="Documentation Best Practice">
Create a single fuel management binder or digital file that consolidates all fuel-related documentation. Organize it by regulatory program (SPCC, NFPA, fuel quality) with a master index. This simplifies both internal audits and regulatory inspections.
</Callout>

## Building a Compliant Fuel Management Program

An effective fuel management program integrates all of the regulatory requirements discussed above into a cohesive operational framework:

1. **Calculate your aggregate oil storage capacity** and determine whether the SPCC rule applies to your facility
2. **Develop or update your SPCC plan** with PE certification if required
3. **Verify your tank construction and installation** meets NFPA 30 and local fire code requirements
4. **Implement a monthly inspection program** covering visual checks, water bottom draining, and containment integrity
5. **Establish a fuel testing schedule** per ASTM D975 with semi-annual microbial and annual comprehensive testing
6. **Contract for annual fuel polishing** or install a permanent polishing system for large fuel inventories
7. **Schedule formal tank inspections** per STI SP001 at the appropriate interval for your tank age and condition
8. **Train all relevant personnel** on spill prevention, fuel handling, and emergency response procedures
9. **Maintain consolidated documentation** that is current, accessible, and audit-ready
10. **Review and update annually** to reflect regulatory changes, equipment modifications, and operational experience

Fuel management compliance is not a one-time effort. It requires ongoing attention, regular testing, and systematic documentation. But the investment pays dividends in generator reliability, environmental protection, and regulatory peace of mind.
