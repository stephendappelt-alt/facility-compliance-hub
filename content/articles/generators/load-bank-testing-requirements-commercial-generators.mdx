## Why Load Bank Testing Is Essential for Commercial Generators

Emergency generators in commercial facilities spend the vast majority of their operational life sitting idle, waiting for a power outage that may never come. When these units are exercised monthly, they often run at a fraction of their rated capacity because the connected building loads during testing rarely approach the generator's full output.

This chronic under-loading creates a serious problem. Diesel engines that run for extended periods below about 30% of their rated capacity cannot reach the temperatures needed to burn fuel completely, and they develop a condition known as wet stacking, where unburned fuel builds up in the exhaust system. Over time, this fouls injectors, causes valve guide wear, and can damage pistons, liners, and rings, degrading engine performance. A generator in poor condition from chronic under-loading may struggle to carry its load during an actual emergency -- the exact moment when full performance is most critical.

Load bank testing addresses this problem by applying a controlled, measured electrical load to the generator independent of the building's connected loads. It is good maintenance practice, and under [NFPA 110](/generators/nfpa-110-emergency-generator-compliance-guide) it becomes a requirement when a diesel generator's monthly tests cannot reach the minimum load.

## What Is a Load Bank

A load bank is a device that provides an artificial electrical load to a generator. It converts the generator's electrical output into heat, which is dissipated through resistive elements and cooling fans. Load banks come in portable and stationary configurations and are available in a wide range of sizes, from small units to multi-megawatt installations.

There are three primary types of load banks used in commercial generator testing:

### Resistive Load Banks

Resistive load banks apply a purely resistive load to the generator, testing the engine and alternator at a specific kilowatt (kW) output. This is the most common type used for routine testing and NFPA 110 compliance. Resistive load banks test the generator at unity power factor (1.0 PF), meaning the load consists entirely of real power.

### Reactive Load Banks

Reactive load banks apply an inductive (kVAR) load that forces the generator to produce reactive power. This tests the alternator's ability to maintain voltage regulation under lagging power factor conditions, which more closely simulates real-world building loads that include motors, transformers, and other inductive equipment.

### Combined Resistive-Reactive Load Banks

These units combine both resistive and reactive elements to test the generator at a specific power factor, commonly 0.8 PF, the power factor at which generator sets are typically rated. Combined testing provides the most thorough evaluation of both engine and alternator performance.

<Callout type="info" title="Which Type Do You Need?">
For routine NFPA 110 site testing, a resistive load bank is commonly used; NFPA 110 site tests can be run at unity power factor if the manufacturer tested the complete unit at its 0.8 PF rated load before shipment. Reactive or combined testing is often chosen for commissioning, post-overhaul verification, and facilities with large motor loads where power factor performance is critical.
</Callout>

## NFPA 110 Load Testing Requirements

NFPA 110 establishes specific requirements for generator load testing that facility managers must understand and follow. The section numbers below are from the 2010 edition, which CMS enforces for healthcare facilities through NFPA 99-2012; the testing rules are similar in later editions, but confirm the numbering in the edition your AHJ has adopted.

### Monthly Exercise Testing -- Section 8.4.2

The standard requires monthly exercise of the EPSS under the following conditions:

- The generator must run under load for a minimum of **30 minutes**
- A diesel unit must carry a load of at least **30% of its nameplate kW rating**, or a load that maintains the **minimum exhaust gas temperature recommended by the manufacturer**
- Healthcare facilities must run the monthly test 12 times a year at intervals of not less than 20 days and not more than 40 days

The 30% figure matches what engine makers say about wet stacking: below roughly 30% load for extended periods, a diesel engine cannot sustain the temperatures needed to burn its fuel completely.

### Annual Supplemental Load Test -- Section 8.4.2.3

A load bank test is not required every year for every generator. It becomes required when monthly tests do not reach the minimum load. **If a diesel unit's monthly tests do not reach 30% of nameplate or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust temperature**, it must still be exercised monthly with the available load, and once a year it must be run with supplemental load for 1.5 continuous hours:

1. **At least 50% of nameplate kW for 30 continuous minutes**, followed by
2. **At least 75% of nameplate kW for 60 continuous minutes**

Building load may serve as part of the load, with a load bank supplying the rest.

Some owners and contractors add a step at 100% of nameplate to confirm full rated capacity. That is a reasonable practice, but NFPA 110 does not require it.

### 36-Month Test -- Section 8.4.9

Level 1 systems must also be tested at least once every 36 months for a minimum of 4 continuous hours (or the duration of the system's Class, if that is shorter), at not less than 30% of nameplate kW or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust gas temperature for diesel units. The annual supplemental test and the 36-month test may be combined: the first 3 hours at not less than 30% of nameplate (or the minimum exhaust temperature) and the final hour at not less than 75% of nameplate. See our [36-month 4-hour load test guide](/generators/generator-36-month-4-hour-load-test-guide) for planning details.

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">
The loading steps above are the minimums NFPA 110 sets when supplemental load testing is required. Your AHJ, accreditor, insurer, or manufacturer may require more. Always confirm the required procedure and the NFPA 110 edition being enforced before scheduling a load bank test.
</Callout>

## The Problem of Wet Stacking

Wet stacking deserves detailed attention because it is the main mechanical reason for load bank testing and a common cause of generator underperformance.

### How Wet Stacking Occurs

When a diesel generator runs at less than about 30% of its capacity for extended periods, the engine cannot sustain the operating temperatures needed to burn its fuel completely. Kohler describes wet stacking (also called engine slobber) as a buildup of unburned fuel in the exhaust system. It typically happens when a generator is oversized for its load or when adequate load is not available during exercise. Common signs include:

- **Exhaust outlet** -- Black ooze or liquid dripping from the exhaust
- **Exhaust smoke** -- Continuous black smoke during operation
- **Soot deposits** -- Buildup of soot in and around the exhaust system
- **Performance** -- Reduced engine performance during loaded operation

### Consequences of Wet Stacking

Wet stacking does not usually damage an engine in the short term, but over time it leads to poor performance and reduced engine life:

1. **Fouled injectors** -- Deposits degrade fuel injection and combustion
2. **Engine wear** -- Kohler lists excessive valve guide wear and possible damage to pistons, liners, and rings
3. **Exhaust fires** -- In extreme cases, accumulated material in the exhaust can ignite when high exhaust temperatures are applied quickly
4. **Emissions equipment** -- Newer engines with diesel oxidation catalysts, SCR systems, or particulate filters are more sensitive to light-load operation; low-temperature running can risk damage to aftertreatment or plug a particulate filter
5. **Higher repair costs** -- Advanced cases may need professional cleaning or an engine overhaul

### How Load Bank Testing Helps

Running a generator at a substantial load for a sustained period raises exhaust temperatures enough to burn off accumulated fuel and deposits. Generator Source, for example, recommends running an early-stage wet-stacked unit at its designed operating temperature with a 75% load. A properly executed load bank test also:

- Clears exhaust system residue through sustained high-temperature operation
- Confirms that the engine can reach and hold the tested load
- Produces a documented record of engine and alternator performance under load

If your generator has aftertreatment equipment, follow the manufacturer's guidance on loading and regeneration, because it may differ from older engines.

<Callout type="tip" title="Visual Indicator">
During a load bank test, it is common to see black smoke from the exhaust during the initial loading phase as accumulated deposits burn off. The smoke should clear as the test progresses. If heavy black smoke persists at high load, have a technician investigate for a mechanical or fuel system problem.
</Callout>

## How to Prepare for a Load Bank Test

Proper preparation ensures the load bank test proceeds safely and produces valid results. The following checklist covers the essential pre-test requirements:

### Pre-Test Preparation Checklist

**Engine and Generator:**
- Verify engine oil level and condition; change oil if due or overdue
- Check coolant level and concentration against the engine manufacturer's specification
- Inspect and replace air filters if dirty or due for replacement
- Verify fuel level -- a loaded test consumes substantial fuel
- Check battery terminal voltage and connections
- Inspect belts, hoses, and visible wiring for damage
- Confirm the engine heater is operating and keeping the engine at the manufacturer's standby temperature

**Fuel System:**
- Ensure adequate fuel supply for the full test duration plus a reserve margin
- Verify fuel quality -- if fuel has not been tested recently, consider a sample analysis before subjecting the engine to full-load operation
- Check fuel filters and replace if due

**Load Bank Setup:**
- Confirm load bank capacity (plus any building load you will use) is enough for the highest planned load step
- Verify cable sizing is appropriate for the generator's rated current output
- Confirm load bank placement allows adequate airflow for heat dissipation
- Verify connection points and ensure proper phase rotation
- Confirm the load bank has functioning overcurrent protection

**Documentation:**
- Prepare test forms that capture load level (kW), voltage, current, frequency, oil pressure, coolant temperature, and ambient conditions at each load step
- Document the generator nameplate data including kW rating, voltage, and power factor
- Record starting conditions before applying load

### During the Test

Monitor the following parameters throughout the test:

- **Voltage** -- Should remain within the manufacturer's regulation tolerance for rated voltage across all load steps
- **Frequency** -- Should remain within the manufacturer's tolerance for rated frequency (60 Hz in the United States)
- **Oil pressure** -- Must remain within the manufacturer's specified range
- **Coolant temperature** -- Must stay within the normal operating range; watch for overheating at full load
- **Exhaust temperature** -- Elevated but stable; monitor for abnormal spikes
- **Fuel pressure** -- Verify adequate fuel delivery at full load
- **Load bank operation** -- Confirm load steps are applying correctly and load bank fans are operating

## Record-Keeping Requirements

NFPA 110 expects test data and readings to be recorded, and CMS surveyors look for written records of maintenance and testing that are maintained and readily available. For load bank testing, your records should include:

- Date and time of test
- Name and qualifications of the testing technician
- Generator identification (make, model, serial number, kW rating)
- Ambient temperature and weather conditions
- Fuel level at start and end of test
- Operating parameters at each load step (kW, voltage, amperage, frequency, oil pressure, coolant temp)
- Duration at each load step
- Total test duration
- Any abnormalities or deficiencies observed
- Black smoke conditions and when they cleared
- Pass or fail determination based on the generator's ability to reach and hold each required load step
- Corrective actions required and completion dates

Keep these records on-site and available for AHJ or surveyor review. NFPA 110 does not set a single retention period that fits every facility, so confirm how long your AHJ, accreditor, and any air permit require you to keep them. At minimum, keep enough history to show your most recent annual and 36-month tests.

## Cost Expectations and Scheduling

Load bank testing is a specialized service that requires equipment, qualified technicians, and coordination with facility operations.

### Typical Cost Factors

The cost of a load bank test depends on several variables:

- **Generator size** -- Larger generators require larger load banks and more fuel, increasing the cost
- **Test duration** -- A 1.5-hour annual supplemental test, a 4-hour 36-month test, or a combined test each require different amounts of technician time
- **Load bank type** -- Resistive-only testing is less expensive than combined resistive-reactive testing
- **Mobilization** -- Transporting a portable load bank to your site is a significant cost component, especially for remote locations
- **Fuel consumption** -- Published estimates put a 500 kW diesel generator at roughly 18.5 gallons per hour at half load, about 26 gallons per hour at three-quarter load, and about 36 gallons per hour at full load; check your engine's spec sheet for actual figures
- **Number of units** -- Sites with multiple generators or paralleled systems need more setup and coordination

Prices vary widely by region, generator size, and contractor, so get written quotes that spell out the load steps, durations, fuel, and documentation included.

### Scheduling Considerations

- **Book early** -- Schedule well ahead of your due date so contractor availability does not push you past your testing interval.
- **Coordinate with operations** -- Decide in advance how the building will be served if the utility fails while the generator is connected to the load bank, and brief staff on that plan. Arrange temporary backup power if your risk assessment calls for it.
- **Weather** -- Outdoor load bank testing should be scheduled during favorable weather. Rain, extreme cold, or extreme heat can affect test conditions and safety.
- **Maintenance first** -- Complete all outstanding preventive maintenance before the load bank test. Testing a generator with known deficiencies wastes time and money.

<Callout type="danger" title="Safety Warning">
Load bank testing involves high-voltage electrical connections and equipment that produces extreme heat. Only qualified technicians with appropriate PPE and safety training should perform load bank connections and operate load bank equipment. Ensure all personnel maintain safe distances from load bank exhaust during operation.
</Callout>

## When Additional Load Bank Testing Is Warranted

Beyond the NFPA 110 requirements, there are several situations where additional load bank testing should be considered:

- **After major engine or alternator repairs** -- Confirm the repaired system performs to its rated capacity
- **After extended periods of inactivity** -- Generators that have not run in several months benefit from a load bank test to clear deposits and verify readiness
- **Following a fuel contamination event** -- After fuel system cleaning, a load test confirms the engine operates correctly on clean fuel
- **When wet stacking symptoms are observed** -- Black exhaust, oily residue at the exhaust outlet, or reduced power output during monthly testing
- **Post-installation commissioning** -- NFPA 110 requires an on-site acceptance test of new installations, which often uses a load bank to supplement building load
- **After a failed emergency start** -- If the generator failed to start or carry load during an actual outage, a thorough evaluation including load bank testing is warranted

## Conclusion

Load bank testing is not just a regulatory requirement -- it is a direct way to verify that your emergency generator can carry substantial load when lives and operations depend on it. Monthly exercises confirm the system will start and transfer. The annual supplemental test, when required, counters the effects of light-load running, and the 36-month test confirms Level 1 systems can run for an extended period.

Build load bank testing into your annual maintenance budget and schedule. Address wet stacking before it degrades performance. Maintain thorough documentation. And work with qualified testing contractors who understand both the mechanical requirements and the code compliance framework.

Your emergency generator exists for one purpose: to deliver reliable power when the utility fails. Load bank testing is how you verify it will fulfill that purpose.
