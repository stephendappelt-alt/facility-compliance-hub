## Understanding the EPA Regulatory Framework for Stationary Generators

Commercial backup generators occupy a unique regulatory space. They are essential life-safety equipment that facility managers hope never to use for extended periods, yet they are also sources of diesel exhaust, particulate matter, and hazardous air pollutants. The EPA regulates these units through overlapping programs that address both the engine hardware and the operational profile of the generator.

If you operate a stationary reciprocating internal combustion engine (RICE) at a commercial facility, federal emissions rules apply whether that engine runs for five hours or five hundred hours per year. The specific obligations depend on the engine's size, fuel type, date of manufacture or installation, and whether it qualifies as an emergency engine.

This guide breaks down the federal rules that apply to most commercial backup generators in the United States, identifies where state rules add additional layers, and explains the practical steps facility managers must take to stay compliant.

## EPA RICE NESHAP: The Foundation of Stationary Engine Regulation

The National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) for Stationary Reciprocating Internal Combustion Engines, codified at 40 CFR Part 63, Subpart ZZZZ, is the primary federal hazardous air pollutant regulation for stationary diesel and gas-fired engines. It is commonly called the RICE NESHAP. It works alongside two New Source Performance Standards (NSPS): 40 CFR Part 60, Subpart IIII for compression-ignition (diesel) engines and Subpart JJJJ for spark-ignition (gas) engines.

### Who Is Covered

The RICE NESHAP applies to stationary RICE at both major and area sources of hazardous air pollutants (HAPs). An area source is any stationary source of HAP that is not a major source, so most commercial and institutional facilities with a backup generator fall under the area source provisions.

For **new or reconstructed** engines at area sources, the RICE NESHAP says compliance is achieved by meeting NSPS Subpart IIII (diesel) or Subpart JJJJ (gas), with no further requirements under Subpart ZZZZ. **Existing** engines at area sources follow the Subpart ZZZZ requirements described below.

### Key Requirements for Existing Emergency Diesel Engines at Area Sources

For existing emergency stationary compression-ignition (CI) engines at area sources, Subpart ZZZZ requires:

- **Operating hour limitations** that define what counts as emergency use (see the next section)
- **Management practices** in Table 2d: change oil and filter every 500 hours of operation or annually; inspect the air cleaner every 1,000 hours or annually; and inspect all hoses and belts every 500 hours or annually, replacing as necessary. "Annually" means within 1 year plus 30 days of the previous service. An oil analysis program may be used to extend oil change intervals.
- **Operation and maintenance** according to the manufacturer's emission-related written instructions or your own maintenance plan
- **A non-resettable hour meter**, installed if one is not already present
- **Records** of hours of operation from the hour meter, separating emergency and non-emergency hours and documenting what made the operation an emergency, plus maintenance records

Existing emergency engines at area sources are not required to submit an initial notification. Ultra-low sulfur diesel (15 ppm nonroad diesel fuel) is required for existing emergency CI engines over 100 hp only if they operate under the limited financial-arrangement provision described below; new engines under NSPS Subpart IIII must use nonroad diesel fuel meeting 40 CFR 1090.305.

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">
Non-emergency engines face stricter requirements, including numerical carbon monoxide limits for larger existing non-emergency diesel engines at area sources. An engine that does not follow the emergency operating limits is not considered an emergency engine and must meet all non-emergency requirements.
</Callout>

## Emergency vs. Non-Emergency Classification

The distinction between emergency and non-emergency generators is the single most consequential classification under EPA rules. It determines which emissions limits apply, how many hours you can operate, and whether you need add-on controls.

### The 100-Hour Rule

Under 40 CFR 63.6675, an emergency stationary RICE is one operated to provide power during an emergency, for example when utility power is interrupted, and otherwise operated only as allowed by 40 CFR 63.6640(f). NSPS Subpart IIII contains parallel limits in 40 CFR 60.4211(f). Under these rules:

- **Emergency operation:** There is no time limit on use during emergency situations.
- **Maintenance and testing:** Up to **100 hours per calendar year** for maintenance checks and readiness testing recommended by government, the manufacturer, the vendor, the grid operator, or the insurer. Owners may petition EPA for more hours, but no petition is needed if records show federal, state, or local standards require more than 100 hours of testing.
- **Non-emergency situations:** Up to **50 hours per calendar year**, and these 50 hours count toward the 100-hour total. They cannot be used for peak shaving, non-emergency demand response, or to generate income by supplying power to the grid, except under a narrow provision for engines at area sources that are dispatched by the local utility to prevent local reliability problems and meet specific conditions.

The rules once allowed emergency engines to run up to 100 hours a year for emergency demand response and during voltage or frequency deviations. A federal appeals court vacated those provisions in 2015, and EPA formally removed them from the regulations in August 2022. Emergency engines can no longer use those allowances.

### Practical Implications of the 100-Hour Limit

The table below is an illustrative example of how a facility might use its maintenance and testing hours. Your actual hours depend on your testing program and the codes that apply to your facility (see the [NFPA 110 compliance guide](/generators/nfpa-110-emergency-generator-compliance-guide) for testing requirements).

| Activity | Example Annual Hours | Notes |
|----------|---------------------|-------|
| Monthly exercise under load (30 min/month) | 6 hours or more | NFPA 110 requires monthly exercise of at least 30 minutes |
| Additional short exercise runs | Varies | Some facilities run brief extra tests |
| Annual supplemental load test | About 1.5 hours | Required by NFPA 110 only if monthly tests do not reach the minimum load |
| 36-month extended test | 4 hours in the test year | Required by NFPA 110 for Level 1 systems |
| Unplanned non-emergency use | Variable | Counts toward the 50-hour sub-limit |

Most facilities operating a generator for true emergency backup will stay well within the 100-hour cap. Facilities that run generators for peak shaving, demand response, or routine power supplementation cannot classify those engines as emergency engines.

<Callout type="info" title="Hour Meter Requirement">
Existing emergency engines at area sources must have a non-resettable hour meter, and new emergency diesel engines under NSPS Subpart IIII that do not meet non-emergency standards must have one installed before startup. Keep records of hours from the meter, separated into emergency and non-emergency operation, with the reason for each emergency run.
</Callout>

## EPA Tier Standards for Diesel Engines

Separate from the RICE NESHAP, EPA sets technology-based emissions standards for new diesel engines. For stationary engines, NSPS Subpart IIII requires 2007 model year and later engines to be certified to the nonroad engine standards in 40 CFR Part 1039 (the "Tier" standards). The standards apply at the point of manufacture.

### Which Tier Applies to Your Generator

A key point that is often misunderstood: **emergency stationary diesel engines are not required to meet Tier 4 standards under federal rules.** Tier 4 applies to non-emergency engines.

| Engine Type (2007+ model year, under 10 liters per cylinder) | Federal Certification Standard | Citation |
|----------|---------------|---------------|
| Emergency engine, 37 kW (50 hp) to 2,237 kW (3,000 hp), not a fire pump | Tier 2 or Tier 3 nonroad standards for the same rated power | 40 CFR 60.4202(a)(2) |
| Emergency engine above 2,237 kW (3,000 hp), 2011 model year and later | Tier 2 nonroad standards | 40 CFR 60.4202(b)(2) |
| Non-emergency engine up to 2,237 kW (3,000 hp) | Current nonroad standards for the model year and power, including Tier 4 | 40 CFR 60.4201(a) |
| Fire pump engine | Separate table of standards (Table 4 to Subpart IIII) | 40 CFR 60.4205(c) |

Owners and operators of 2007 and later emergency engines must meet the standards for the same model year and power rating. Some state and local air agencies require cleaner engines than the federal minimum, so check your permit conditions.

### Tier 4 Final Technology

Tier 4 Final engines, used for non-emergency applications or where a state or local permit requires them, typically incorporate:

- **Diesel Particulate Filter (DPF):** Captures soot and requires periodic regeneration, either passive (exhaust heat) or active
- **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR):** Injects diesel exhaust fluid (DEF) to convert NOx into nitrogen and water
- **Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR):** Recirculates a portion of exhaust gas to reduce combustion temperatures and NOx formation
- **Electronic engine controls:** Engine management systems that optimize combustion and manage aftertreatment

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">
Engines with DPF and SCR systems require regular DEF replenishment and periodic DPF maintenance. Under NSPS Subpart IIII, a DPF installed to meet the standards must have a backpressure monitor, and owners must keep records of corrective action taken when the high-backpressure warning is approached. Include these items in your preventive maintenance program.
</Callout>

## State Implementation Plan Overlays

The Clean Air Act allows states to adopt requirements that are stricter than federal standards. Many states and local air districts impose additional constraints on stationary generators through permits and rules.

### Common State-Level Additions

Requirements you may encounter include:

- **Lower annual hour limits** for maintenance and testing (California's limits are described below)
- **Permits to operate** or registrations for emergency generators above certain size thresholds
- **Best Available Control Technology (BACT)** requirements for new installations, especially in nonattainment areas
- **Registration and fee requirements** for stationary engines
- **Source testing** for larger or non-emergency engines
- **Opacity limits** on visible emissions during operation

Check with your state environmental agency and, where applicable, your local air district to determine the full set of requirements that apply. Federal compliance alone may not be sufficient.

## California ATCM: A Stricter State Standard

California's Airborne Toxic Control Measure (ATCM) for Stationary Compression Ignition Engines (17 CCR 93115), administered by the California Air Resources Board (CARB) and enforced by local air districts, is one of the most restrictive state rules for diesel backup generators.

### California ATCM Key Provisions

Based on CARB's final regulation order (amendments effective May 19, 2011):

- **New emergency standby diesel engines over 50 bhp** are limited to **50 hours per year** for maintenance and testing. A district may allow up to 100 hours on a site-specific basis if diesel PM emissions are 0.01 g/bhp-hr or less.
- **New emergency standby engines** must meet CARB's emission standards table and be certified to the federal NSPS Subpart IIII standards for their model year.
- **In-use (existing) emergency standby engines** are limited to 20 or 30 hours per year of maintenance and testing depending on their diesel PM emission rate, with districts able to approve more hours in certain cases, including for health facilities.
- **Near schools**, emergency standby engines generally may not be run for maintenance and testing during school activities or school hours within 500 feet of school grounds, unless PM emissions are 0.01 g/bhp-hr or less.

Local air districts may impose additional requirements through permits, so California facilities should confirm limits with their district.

## Emissions Testing and Reporting Requirements

### Source Testing

Emergency engines that meet the emergency operating limits generally do not require periodic performance testing under the federal rules described above. Non-emergency engines, larger engines, and engines covered by certain state or local permits may be required to conduct source (stack) testing. Where required, testing typically involves:

- Hiring a qualified third-party testing firm
- Testing for the pollutants named in the rule or permit, such as CO, NOx, or PM
- Submitting results to the permitting authority within the deadline in the rule or permit
- Repeating tests at the interval the rule or permit specifies

### Ongoing Recordkeeping

For emergency generators subject to the RICE NESHAP, ongoing compliance includes:

- Maintaining an **operations log** from the hour meter showing emergency and non-emergency hours and the reason for emergency operation
- Recording **maintenance activities** as they are performed
- Retaining **fuel records** where ultra-low sulfur fuel is required
- Submitting **reports** if required by your permit, or any reports the rule requires if the engine is used under the limited financial-arrangement provision

Under 40 CFR 63.6660, records must be kept for **5 years** following each occurrence and be readily accessible for review.

## Retrofit and Replacement Options for Older Generators

Facilities operating older generators may face pressure to reduce emissions, particularly in nonattainment areas and states with aggressive air quality programs. The primary options are:

### Engine Retrofit

Retrofitting involves adding aftertreatment equipment to an existing engine:

- **Diesel Oxidation Catalysts (DOC):** Reduce CO and hydrocarbons, with more modest PM reductions
- **Diesel Particulate Filters (DPF):** Provide large PM reductions but require adequate exhaust temperature for regeneration
- **SCR systems:** Reduce NOx but require DEF storage and dosing infrastructure

Actual reductions depend on the specific verified product and engine. Retrofit feasibility also depends on available space, exhaust backpressure tolerances, and the engine's controls. Not all older engines can accommodate modern aftertreatment without significant modification.

### Engine Repower

Repowering replaces the engine within an existing generator package with a newer certified engine. This can preserve the alternator, switchgear, enclosure, and fuel system while improving emissions performance. Confirm with your air agency how a repowered engine will be classified (new or reconstructed) before proceeding, since that affects which rules apply.

### Full Replacement

Replacing the entire generator set provides the newest technology, including modern electronic controls, improved fuel efficiency, and full manufacturer warranty coverage. A new engine must meet the standards in effect for its model year, which may simplify permitting.

Costs for each option vary widely with engine size, site conditions, and local requirements, so get quotes from qualified contractors and discuss options with your air agency.

<Callout type="tip" title="Financial Incentives">
Some state and local air quality agencies offer grant funding or incentive programs for voluntary early replacement or retrofit of older diesel engines. Check with your regional air district for available programs before committing to a compliance pathway.
</Callout>

## Building a Compliance Program

Maintaining EPA compliance for your commercial backup generators requires a structured, ongoing program rather than a one-time effort. The essential elements include:

1. **Engine inventory:** Document each generator's make, model, serial number, model year, EPA certification, horsepower, fuel type, and date of installation
2. **Classification verification:** Confirm emergency vs. non-emergency status for each unit and document the basis for the classification
3. **Hour tracking:** Record operating hours from the non-resettable hour meter, categorized as emergency, maintenance/testing, or other non-emergency, with the reason for each emergency run
4. **Maintenance documentation:** Keep detailed records of all preventive maintenance performed, including the Table 2d management practices for existing engines
5. **Fuel compliance:** Retain fuel delivery records confirming the fuel meets applicable specifications
6. **Permit review:** Periodically review state and local operating permits for updated conditions or reporting deadlines
7. **Regulatory monitoring:** Track proposed federal and state regulatory changes that may affect your generators

Designating a specific individual as the responsible party for generator compliance and building these tasks into your maintenance tracking system will help prevent oversights that lead to violations. For how emissions rules intersect with fuel storage, see our guide to [generator fuel management and EPA regulations](/generators/generator-fuel-management-compliance-epa-regulations), and for testing schedules see the [NFPA 110 compliance guide](/generators/nfpa-110-emergency-generator-compliance-guide).

## Key Takeaways

EPA emissions compliance for commercial backup generators spans multiple regulatory programs at the federal, state, and local levels. The RICE NESHAP and the stationary engine NSPS establish operating limits, management practices, and recordkeeping, while the Tier standards govern the emissions performance of the engine hardware itself. Federally, emergency diesel engines are certified to Tier 2 or Tier 3 standards, while Tier 4 applies to non-emergency engines. State overlays, particularly in California, frequently impose additional constraints.

Facility managers should focus on three priorities: correctly classifying each generator as emergency or non-emergency and staying within the 100-hour and 50-hour limits, maintaining meticulous operating and maintenance records, and evaluating retrofit or replacement options for aging units with their air agency. A disciplined compliance program protects your facility from enforcement actions while ensuring your backup power systems are ready to perform when you need them most.
