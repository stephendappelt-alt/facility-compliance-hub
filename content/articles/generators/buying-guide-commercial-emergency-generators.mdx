## Before You Buy: Getting the Decision Right

Purchasing a commercial emergency generator is a capital decision that will affect your facility for decades. The unit you select must meet building code requirements, satisfy your Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), comply with EPA emissions regulations, and reliably power your critical loads during outages that may last hours or days.

This guide walks through the key decisions in a logical sequence: determining how much power you need, choosing a fuel type, selecting the right configuration, evaluating specifications, planning the installation, and understanding the true long-term cost.

## Step 1: Determine Required Generator Capacity

Undersizing a generator means critical systems go dark during an outage. Oversizing wastes capital, increases fuel consumption, and can cause wet stacking from chronic light-load operation. The goal is accurate sizing based on a methodical load analysis.

### Load Analysis Methodology

A proper load analysis involves four stages:

**1. Identify critical loads.** Work with your electrical engineer to classify every load in the facility into one of three categories:

- **Emergency (life-safety) loads** (NEC Article 700; typically served by NFPA 110 Level 1 systems): Fire alarm systems, emergency egress lighting and exit signs, and other loads the codes treat as emergency systems. These are code-mandated and non-negotiable.
- **Legally required standby loads** (NEC Article 701; typically NFPA 110 Level 2): Loads such as certain ventilation, sewage disposal, and communication systems that the building code or AHJ requires.
- **Optional standby loads** (NEC Article 702): IT systems, refrigeration, security systems, selected HVAC, and other business-critical equipment that is not code-required but operationally important.

For more on how these categories are wired and separated, see our guide to [NEC 700, 701, and 702 load classification](/generators/nec-700-701-702-emergency-generator-wiring-load-classification).

**2. Calculate connected load.** For each critical load, determine the nameplate kW or kVA rating. For motor loads, account for starting kVA, which for across-the-line starting is several times the running current, and plan a step-loading sequence.

**3. Apply demand factors.** Not all connected loads operate simultaneously. Your engineer will apply demand factors based on the facility type and, ideally, measured operating data.

**4. Add growth margin.** Agree with your engineer on a capacity margin above the calculated demand to accommodate future load additions without requiring a generator replacement.

<Callout type="info" title="Professional Sizing">
NFPA 110 expects the emergency power supply system to be able to carry the load it is intended to serve. For any facility where the generator will support life-safety loads, engage a licensed professional engineer to perform or review the load analysis. This protects you from both undersizing errors and AHJ challenges to your sizing methodology.
</Callout>

### Sizing Varies Widely by Facility

There is no reliable rule of thumb that converts building type or square footage into generator size. Two buildings of the same size can need very different generators depending on whether the generator carries only life-safety loads or also HVAC, elevators, IT, refrigeration, or process equipment. Healthcare facilities and data centers often need large or multiple generators because of the loads and redundancy they require. Use your load analysis, not a published range, to set capacity.

## Step 2: Choose a Fuel Type

The three primary fuel options for commercial emergency generators each have distinct advantages, limitations, and compliance implications.

### Fuel Type Comparison

| Factor | Diesel | Natural Gas | Bi-Fuel (Diesel + Gas) |
|--------|--------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Fuel storage** | On-site tanks required | Utility pipeline; no on-site storage needed | Requires diesel tank plus gas connection |
| **Fuel reliability** | Independent of utility infrastructure | Dependent on gas supply continuing during the event | Dual supply provides redundancy |
| **Run time** | Limited by tank capacity; easily calculated | Continues as long as gas supply is maintained | Extended run time vs. diesel alone |
| **Emissions** | Federal emergency-engine standards (Tier 2 or 3 for most sizes); state or local rules may require more | Covered by separate spark-ignition engine rules; permitting varies | Diesel engine rules apply; check with your air agency |
| **Cold weather starting** | Reliable with proper engine heating | Requires adequate gas pressure and regulation | Starts on diesel |
| **Maintenance** | Well-understood service requirements plus fuel quality management | No stored-fuel management | More complex controls and gas train |
| **NFPA 110 considerations** | On-site storage sized to the EPSS Class | AHJ must accept the reliability of the gas supply | Diesel tank provides backup to gas supply |

Capital, fuel, and maintenance costs vary widely by size, region, and site conditions, so compare engineered quotes rather than published averages.

### Diesel Generators

Diesel remains the most common fuel choice for emergency generators in commercial facilities. On-site fuel storage provides independence from utility infrastructure, and the technology is mature with a deep bench of qualified service providers.

A common misconception is that every new diesel generator must meet Tier 4 Final. Under the federal stationary engine rules (40 CFR Part 60, Subpart IIII), 2007 and later **emergency** diesel engines are certified to Tier 2 or Tier 3 nonroad standards for their size, and Tier 4 applies to non-emergency engines. Some state and local air agencies require cleaner engines or add permit conditions, and Tier 4 Final engines with diesel particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction add complexity and maintenance. See our guide to [EPA emissions standards for backup generators](/generators/epa-emissions-standards-commercial-backup-generators).

Fuel storage requires compliance with NFPA 30 (Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code) and, where the facility's aggregate aboveground oil storage exceeds 1,320 gallons (counting containers of 55 gallons or more) and a discharge could reach navigable waters, EPA's Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure (SPCC) rule. See our guide to [generator fuel management and EPA regulations](/generators/generator-fuel-management-compliance-epa-regulations).

### Natural Gas Generators

Natural gas generators eliminate on-site fuel storage and produce much lower particulate emissions than diesel, which can simplify air quality permitting in some jurisdictions. They are attractive for facilities in urban areas where large diesel tanks pose logistical or regulatory challenges.

The primary concern with natural gas is supply reliability. During widespread natural disasters, gas pressure may drop or service may be interrupted. For Level 1 systems in particular, some AHJs expect on-site fuel storage.

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">
Before selecting natural gas, confirm with your AHJ that a pipeline-supplied generator meets their fuel supply reliability requirements for your occupancy type. Some jurisdictions require on-site fuel storage for Level 1 systems, which may favor diesel or bi-fuel configurations.
</Callout>

### Bi-Fuel Generators

Bi-fuel generators start and run on diesel, then introduce natural gas to displace a portion of the diesel fuel consumption once the engine is running under load. The displacement ratio depends on the manufacturer, engine, and load, so ask the vendor for its rated figures.

Bi-fuel offers extended run time beyond the diesel tank capacity and reduced diesel consumption during prolonged outages. However, the systems are more complex, with additional controls, gas trains, and piping that increase both capital and maintenance costs.

## Step 3: Single Unit vs. Paralleling Systems

For large loads, or for facilities requiring high availability, paralleling multiple smaller generators offers advantages over a single large unit.

| Factor | Single Unit | Paralleled System (2+ units) |
|--------|------------|------------------------------|
| **Redundancy** | Single point of failure | N+1 configurations possible |
| **Load matching** | Fixed capacity; potential for light loading | Units brought online as needed; better load matching |
| **Maintenance** | Entire system offline during service | Service one unit while others carry load (if sized for N+1) |
| **Footprint** | Single pad, single exhaust | Multiple pads, more complex routing |
| **Capital cost** | Generally lower for equivalent total kW | Higher due to paralleling switchgear and controls |
| **Fuel efficiency** | Best near rated load | Better across a wider load range |
| **Permitting** | Single unit to permit | May require permits for each unit |

Paralleling is common in hospitals, data centers, and other mission-critical facilities. For many commercial office and retail applications, a single properly sized generator is the cost-effective choice.

## Step 4: Key Specifications to Evaluate

When comparing generator proposals from different vendors, focus on these specifications:

### Engine

- **Manufacturer and support:** Several major manufacturers build commercial generator engines. Availability of local service and parts is often the deciding factor.
- **EPA certification:** Confirm the engine is certified to the standard that applies to its use (emergency or non-emergency) and model year, plus any stricter state or local requirement. Get the certification documentation.
- **Governing system:** Electronic governing is standard on modern units and provides tighter frequency regulation than mechanical governors.
- **Starting system:** Verify the batteries are sized to complete NFPA 110's cycle-cranking sequence of 15 seconds cranking and 15 seconds rest, repeated three times, at the site's worst-case temperature.

### Alternator

- **Manufacturer:** Several major alternator manufacturers supply the generator packagers; compare the specific model offered.
- **Winding pitch:** A 2/3 pitch winding reduces third-harmonic distortion, which can matter for facilities with significant nonlinear or sensitive electronic loads.
- **Insulation class and temperature rise:** Confirm the insulation class and the rated temperature rise, which affect thermal margin and alternator life.
- **Voltage regulation:** Compare the manufacturer's steady-state voltage regulation specification between proposals.

### Control System

- **Digital controller:** Modern generator controllers provide engine management, alternator protection, transfer switch communication, and remote monitoring in a single platform. For Level 1 systems, NFPA 110 specifies required safety indications and shutdowns, so confirm the controller provides them.
- **Remote monitoring:** Cellular or network-connected monitoring provides real-time alerts for fault conditions, low fuel, and maintenance reminders.
- **Load management:** Controllers with programmable load shed and add sequencing protect the generator from overload during initial transfer.

## Step 5: Sound Attenuation and Enclosures

Generator noise is usually specified in decibels (dBA) at a stated distance from the enclosure, commonly 7 meters. Local noise ordinances and the proximity of neighboring properties will determine the required attenuation level.

Enclosure options generally range from open skid-mounted units in mechanical rooms, to standard weather-protective enclosures, to sound-attenuated enclosures offered in several levels. Manufacturers use different names and ratings for these levels, so compare the published sound level at the same distance and load across proposals. Higher attenuation adds cost, size, and weight, but failing to meet local noise ordinances after installation is far more expensive to resolve.

<Callout type="tip" title="Noise Assessment">
Before specifying an enclosure, determine the applicable noise limit at your property line and the distance from the proposed generator location to the nearest sensitive receptor. Your generator vendor or an acoustical consultant can then recommend the minimum enclosure level needed to comply. Over-specifying sound attenuation wastes money; under-specifying creates a problem that is expensive to fix after the fact.
</Callout>

## Step 6: Installation Considerations

The generator itself is only part of the project. Installation, electrical work, and permitting can be a large share of the total cost. Plan for the following:

### AHJ Permitting

- **Building permit** for the concrete pad, enclosure, and structural supports
- **Electrical permit** for the generator, transfer switches, and associated distribution wiring
- **Mechanical permit** for fuel piping, exhaust systems, and ventilation
- **Fire department review** for fuel storage, fire suppression (if required), and separation distances
- **Air quality permit or registration** from the state or local air agency, where required for the engine
- **Noise variance** if the installation cannot meet standard noise limits

Permitting timelines vary widely by jurisdiction, so ask your engineer and local officials early and build the time into your schedule.

### Site Preparation

- **Concrete pad:** Designed by your engineer for the generator weight and footprint plus service clearance, with vibration isolation as specified by the manufacturer.
- **Fuel system:** Aboveground or underground tanks with secondary containment, fill connections, vents, and leak detection as required by NFPA 30, the fire code, and EPA rules.
- **Exhaust routing:** Exhaust piping from the engine to the discharge point, including a flexible connection to isolate engine vibration, rain protection, and routing away from building air intakes.
- **Ventilation:** Combustion air supply and radiator cooling air discharge paths. Generator rooms require calculated airflow to prevent overheating and ensure adequate combustion air. See our guide to [generator room design](/generators/generator-room-design-nfpa-37-ventilation-clearances).

### Electrical Integration

- **Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS):** Sized for the load it serves and rated for the available fault current. For emergency systems, the NEC requires transfer equipment to be automatic, identified for emergency use, and approved by the AHJ. See our [ATS maintenance guide](/generators/automatic-transfer-switch-maintenance-nfpa-110).
- **Distribution wiring:** Conductors sized for generator output current with appropriate voltage drop calculations for the run length.
- **Grounding:** Generator grounding and bonding per NFPA 70 (National Electrical Code) Article 250.

## Step 7: Maintenance Approach

How you maintain the generator is as important as what you buy. The two primary approaches are service contracts with the generator dealer or distributor, and in-house maintenance performed by facility staff.

### Maintenance Approach Comparison

| Factor | Service Contract | In-House Maintenance |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Cost structure** | Fixed annual fee; predictable budgeting | Parts and labor as needed; variable |
| **Expertise** | Technicians trained on the equipment | Depends on staff training and experience |
| **Response time** | Defined in contract | Immediate for trained staff; varies |
| **Parts availability** | Dealer maintains inventory | Must stock critical spares or wait for delivery |
| **Documentation** | Provided by service provider | Facility must maintain own records |
| **Load bank testing** | Typically included or available as add-on | Must rent or purchase load bank equipment |
| **Best for** | Facilities without dedicated mechanical staff | Facilities with trained engineers on staff |

Many facilities use a hybrid approach: in-house staff perform weekly inspections and monthly exercise tests, while a service contractor handles periodic maintenance and load testing that require specialized tools and expertise. Either way, the facility remains responsible for meeting NFPA 110 and EPA requirements. See our [weekly, monthly, and annual maintenance schedule](/generators/weekly-monthly-annual-generator-maintenance-schedule).

<Callout type="info" title="Contract Essentials">
If you choose a service contract, make sure it spells out what is covered: fluids and filters, any required supplemental load bank testing, transfer switch inspection, battery testing and replacement, emergency response, and detailed maintenance documentation provided to you after each service visit. Get the response time commitment in writing.
</Callout>

## Step 8: Total Cost of Ownership Analysis

The purchase price of the generator is only part of the first-year cost and a smaller fraction of the lifetime cost. A complete total cost of ownership (TCO) analysis should include all of the following elements.

### TCO Framework

| Cost Category | What to Include |
|--------------|-----------------|
| Generator package | Engine, alternator, controller, enclosure |
| Transfer switches | Number, amperage, and features |
| Installation (mechanical) | Pad, fuel system, exhaust, ventilation |
| Installation (electrical) | Wiring, conduit, grounding, integration |
| Permitting and engineering | Design, permits, inspections, commissioning |
| Annual fuel | Testing hours plus expected outage hours |
| Annual maintenance | Contract or in-house parts and labor, fuel testing, load bank testing |
| Emissions compliance | Permits, fees, and aftertreatment maintenance where applicable |
| Major repairs and overhaul | Engine and alternator work over the equipment's life |

Get installed pricing for each line from vendors rather than relying on averages, because costs vary widely by size, region, and site conditions. The lowest equipment price often comes with the highest installation cost due to non-standard configurations or missing accessories.

## Questions to Ask Vendors Before Purchasing

Before signing a purchase order, get clear answers to each of these questions:

1. **What is the total installed price**, including pad, fuel system, transfer switches, wiring, permitting, and commissioning?
2. **What is the lead time** from order to operational commissioning?
3. **What warranty is included**, and what does it cover? What are the warranty conditions (e.g., required maintenance by authorized dealer)?
4. **Where is the nearest authorized service center**, and what is the guaranteed emergency response time?
5. **What parts are stocked locally** versus ordered from regional or national distribution?
6. **What is the expected fuel consumption** at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% load?
7. **What is the noise level** at the specified measurement distance, and what enclosure options are available?
8. **Which EPA standard is the engine certified to** (emergency or non-emergency, and which Tier), does that meet any state or local requirement at my site, and can you provide the certification documentation?
9. **What remote monitoring capability** is included or available, and what are the ongoing subscription costs?
10. **What are the maintenance intervals and estimated annual maintenance costs** for the first five years?
11. **Can you provide references** from facilities of similar size and type in this geographic area?
12. **What training** is provided to facility staff upon commissioning?

## Making the Final Decision

The right generator for your facility is the one that reliably powers your critical loads, meets all applicable codes and regulations, fits your site constraints, and delivers the lowest total cost of ownership over its service life.

Resist the temptation to make the decision solely on equipment price. A generator that costs less up front but requires more expensive maintenance, has slower emergency response from the nearest dealer, or needs a noise enclosure upgrade after installation is not actually cheaper.

Invest the time in a proper load analysis, compare total installed costs, verify local service infrastructure, and confirm code compliance with your AHJ before the equipment arrives. The decisions you make during the purchasing process determine the compliance posture and operational reliability of your emergency power system for years to come.
