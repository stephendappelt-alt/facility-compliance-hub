## Before You Buy: Getting the Decision Right

Purchasing a commercial emergency generator is a capital decision that will affect your facility for 20 to 30 years. The unit you select must meet building code requirements, satisfy your Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), comply with EPA emissions regulations, and reliably power your critical loads during outages that may last hours or days.

This guide walks through the key decisions in a logical sequence: determining how much power you need, choosing a fuel type, selecting the right configuration, evaluating specifications, planning the installation, and understanding the true long-term cost.

## Step 1: Determine Required Generator Capacity

Undersizing a generator means critical systems go dark during an outage. Oversizing wastes capital, increases fuel consumption, and can cause wet stacking from chronic light-load operation. The goal is accurate sizing based on a methodical load analysis.

### Load Analysis Methodology

A proper load analysis involves four stages:

**1. Identify critical loads.** Work with your electrical engineer to classify every load in the facility into one of three categories:

- **Life-safety loads** (NFPA 110 Level 1): Fire alarm systems, emergency egress lighting, fire pump(s), smoke control systems, elevator recall. These are code-mandated and non-negotiable.
- **Legally required standby loads** (NFPA 110 Level 2): Building ventilation, sewage pumps, communication systems, and other loads required by the AHJ.
- **Optional standby loads:** IT systems, refrigeration, security systems, selected HVAC, and other business-critical equipment that is not code-required but operationally important.

**2. Calculate connected load.** For each critical load, determine the nameplate kW or kVA rating. For motor loads, account for the starting kVA (typically 6x the running current for across-the-line starting) using a step-loading sequence.

**3. Apply demand factors.** Not all connected loads operate simultaneously. Apply appropriate demand factors based on the facility type and historical operating data. Typical demand factors range from 0.70 to 0.90 for most commercial facilities.

**4. Add growth margin.** Include 10-20% capacity above the calculated demand load to accommodate future load additions without requiring a generator replacement.

<Callout type="info" title="Professional Sizing">
NFPA 110 Section 5.1 requires that the EPSS be sized to handle the maximum load expected to be connected. For any facility where the generator will support life-safety loads, engage a licensed professional engineer to perform or review the load analysis. This protects you from both undersizing errors and AHJ challenges to your sizing methodology.
</Callout>

### Common Sizing Ranges by Facility Type

| Facility Type | Typical Generator Size | Key Load Drivers |
|--------------|----------------------|------------------|
| Small office building (20,000-50,000 sq ft) | 100-250 kW | Lighting, HVAC, elevators, IT |
| Medium office / mixed-use (50,000-150,000 sq ft) | 250-750 kW | Multiple elevators, HVAC, data center |
| Hospital / healthcare | 500-3,000+ kW | Full facility load, surgical suites, life support |
| Data center | 1,000-10,000+ kW | IT load, cooling, redundancy requirements |
| Retail / grocery | 200-500 kW | Refrigeration, lighting, POS systems |
| Manufacturing | 500-5,000+ kW | Process equipment, compressors, material handling |
| Assisted living / nursing home | 150-500 kW | Life safety, HVAC, medical equipment, kitchen |

These are rough ranges. Your actual requirement depends entirely on your load analysis results.

## Step 2: Choose a Fuel Type

The three primary fuel options for commercial emergency generators each have distinct advantages, limitations, and compliance implications.

### Fuel Type Comparison

| Factor | Diesel | Natural Gas | Bi-Fuel (Diesel + Gas) |
|--------|--------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Power density** | Highest kW per dollar | Lower; larger engine for same output | Matches diesel on diesel; reduced on gas |
| **Fuel storage** | On-site tanks required (500-10,000+ gallons) | Utility pipeline; no on-site storage needed | Requires diesel tank plus gas connection |
| **Fuel reliability** | Independent of utility infrastructure | Dependent on gas pipeline integrity | Dual supply provides redundancy |
| **Run time** | Limited by tank capacity; easily calculated | Unlimited as long as pipeline supplies gas | Extended run time vs. diesel alone |
| **Emissions (EPA Tier)** | Subject to Tier 4 Final standards | Lower NOx and PM; may have simpler permitting | Reduced diesel consumption lowers PM output |
| **Cold weather starting** | Excellent with block heater | Good; requires gas pressure regulation | Starts on diesel; reliable cold start |
| **Maintenance cost** | Moderate; well-understood service requirements | Lower; fewer particulates, longer oil life | Slightly higher than diesel alone |
| **Capital cost (500 kW)** | $150,000-$250,000 | $175,000-$300,000 | $200,000-$325,000 |
| **Fuel cost per kWh** | $0.25-$0.35 | $0.15-$0.25 | $0.18-$0.28 (blended) |
| **NFPA 110 considerations** | On-site storage supports extended runtime guarantees | Pipeline disruption risk during seismic or widespread events | Diesel tank provides backup to gas supply |

### Diesel Generators

Diesel remains the dominant fuel choice for emergency generators in commercial facilities. The reasons are straightforward: diesel engines offer the highest power density per dollar of capital cost, on-site fuel storage guarantees independence from utility infrastructure, and the technology is mature with a deep bench of qualified service providers.

The primary disadvantage of diesel is emissions compliance. Tier 4 Final diesel generators require diesel particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction systems that add complexity and maintenance requirements. In areas with strict air quality regulations, diesel generators may require more extensive permitting.

Fuel storage requires compliance with NFPA 30 (Flammable and Combustible Liquids Code) and EPA SPCC (Spill Prevention, Control, and Countermeasure) regulations for tanks exceeding 1,320 gallons aggregate above-ground storage.

### Natural Gas Generators

Natural gas generators eliminate on-site fuel storage requirements and produce significantly lower particulate emissions than diesel, which simplifies air quality permitting in many jurisdictions. They are particularly attractive for facilities in urban areas where large diesel tanks pose logistical or regulatory challenges.

The primary concern with natural gas is supply reliability. During widespread natural disasters, gas pipeline pressure may drop or service may be interrupted. NFPA 110 requires that the fuel supply for Level 1 systems be reliable enough to support the required operating duration, which some AHJs interpret as requiring on-site fuel storage.

<Callout type="warning" title="Important">
Before selecting natural gas, confirm with your AHJ that a pipeline-supplied generator meets their fuel supply reliability requirements for your occupancy type. Some jurisdictions require on-site fuel storage for Level 1 systems regardless of fuel type, which may favor diesel or bi-fuel configurations.
</Callout>

### Bi-Fuel Generators

Bi-fuel generators start and run on diesel, then introduce natural gas to displace a portion of the diesel fuel consumption once the engine reaches operating temperature. Typical displacement ratios range from 50% to 75% natural gas under steady-state conditions.

Bi-fuel offers extended run time beyond the diesel tank capacity, reduced fuel costs during prolonged outages, and lower overall emissions. However, the systems are more complex, with additional controls, gas trains, and piping that increase both capital and maintenance costs.

## Step 3: Single Unit vs. Paralleling Systems

For generators above approximately 1,000 kW, or for facilities requiring high availability, paralleling multiple smaller generators offers advantages over a single large unit.

| Factor | Single Unit | Paralleled System (2+ units) |
|--------|------------|------------------------------|
| **Redundancy** | Single point of failure | N+1 configurations possible |
| **Load matching** | Fixed capacity; potential for light loading | Units brought online as needed; better load matching |
| **Maintenance** | Entire system offline during service | Service one unit while others carry load |
| **Footprint** | Single pad, single exhaust | Multiple pads, more complex routing |
| **Capital cost** | Lower for equivalent total kW | 20-40% higher due to paralleling switchgear |
| **Fuel efficiency** | Optimized at rated load only | Optimized across a wider load range |
| **Permitting** | Simpler; single unit to permit | May require individual permits per unit |

Paralleling is standard practice in hospitals, data centers, and other mission-critical facilities. For most commercial office and retail applications, a single properly sized generator is the cost-effective choice.

## Step 4: Key Specifications to Evaluate

When comparing generator proposals from different vendors, focus on these specifications:

### Engine

- **Manufacturer reputation:** Caterpillar, Cummins, MTU, Volvo Penta, and John Deere are the dominant commercial engine platforms. All produce reliable engines; availability of local service and parts is often the deciding factor.
- **EPA Tier certification:** Tier 4 Final is required for all new installations. Verify the certification documentation.
- **Governing system:** Electronic governing is standard and provides better frequency regulation than mechanical governors.
- **Starting system:** 24V battery systems are standard for engines above 150 kW. Verify battery sizing meets NFPA 110 cranking cycle requirements (minimum of three complete cranking cycles).

### Alternator

- **Manufacturer:** Stamford, Leroy-Somer, Marathon, and Mecc Alte are the primary alternator manufacturers. All major generator packagers offer high-quality alternator options.
- **Winding pitch:** A 2/3 pitch winding reduces third-harmonic distortion, which is important for facilities with sensitive electronic loads.
- **Insulation class:** Class H insulation (180degC rated) is standard for commercial generators and provides appropriate thermal margin.
- **Voltage regulation:** Digital voltage regulators should maintain output within plus or minus 1% of rated voltage under steady-state conditions.

### Control System

- **Digital controller:** Modern generator controllers provide engine management, alternator protection, ATS communication, and remote monitoring in a single platform.
- **Remote monitoring:** Cellular or network-connected monitoring provides real-time alerts for fault conditions, low fuel, and maintenance reminders.
- **Load management:** Controllers with programmable load shed and add sequencing protect the generator from overload during initial transfer.

## Step 5: Sound Attenuation and Enclosures

Generator noise is measured in decibels at a standard distance, typically 7 meters (23 feet) from the enclosure. Local noise ordinances and the proximity of neighboring properties will determine the required attenuation level.

| Enclosure Level | Typical dBA at 7m | Application |
|----------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Open / skid-mounted | 95-105 dBA | Mechanical rooms with adequate sound isolation |
| Standard weather enclosure | 82-90 dBA | Industrial areas with setback from property lines |
| Level 1 sound attenuated | 75-82 dBA | Commercial areas, moderate setback |
| Level 2 sound attenuated | 68-75 dBA | Urban commercial, near residential |
| Level 3 / hospital grade | 60-68 dBA | Hospitals, dense urban, noise-sensitive neighbors |
| Super-silent / critical | Below 60 dBA | Directly adjacent to residential, recording studios |

Higher attenuation levels add significant cost. A Level 3 enclosure may add $30,000-$80,000 to the generator package price compared to a standard weather enclosure. However, failing to meet local noise ordinances after installation is far more expensive to resolve.

<Callout type="tip" title="Noise Assessment">
Before specifying an enclosure, determine the applicable noise limit at your property line and the distance from the proposed generator location to the nearest sensitive receptor. Your generator vendor can then recommend the minimum enclosure level needed to comply. Over-specifying sound attenuation wastes money; under-specifying creates a problem that is expensive to fix after the fact.
</Callout>

## Step 6: Installation Considerations

The generator itself is only part of the project. Installation costs frequently equal or exceed the equipment cost. Plan for the following:

### AHJ Permitting

- **Building permit** for the concrete pad, enclosure, and structural supports
- **Electrical permit** for the generator, ATS, and associated distribution wiring
- **Mechanical permit** for fuel piping, exhaust systems, and ventilation
- **Fire department review** for fuel storage, fire suppression (if required), and separation distances
- **Air quality permit** from the state or local air district for the stationary engine
- **Noise variance** if the installation cannot meet standard noise limits

Allow 4-12 weeks for permitting depending on the jurisdiction. In some urban markets, permitting can extend to 6 months or more.

### Site Preparation

- **Concrete pad:** Sized for the generator footprint plus service clearance on all sides. Typical pads are 6-inch reinforced concrete on compacted gravel. Include vibration isolation pads between the generator and concrete.
- **Fuel system:** Above-ground storage tanks (ASTs) or below-ground tanks (USTs) with secondary containment, fill connections, vents, and leak detection per NFPA 30 and EPA SPCC requirements.
- **Exhaust routing:** Exhaust piping from the engine to the discharge point, including a flexible connection to isolate engine vibration, rain cap, and routing away from building air intakes.
- **Ventilation:** Combustion air supply and radiator cooling air discharge paths. Generator rooms require calculated airflow to prevent overheating and ensure adequate combustion air.

### Electrical Integration

- **Automatic Transfer Switch (ATS):** Sized for the full generator output, rated for the available fault current, and listed for the application. NFPA 110 requires the ATS to be listed for emergency service.
- **Distribution wiring:** Conductors sized for generator output current with appropriate voltage drop calculations for the run length.
- **Grounding:** Generator grounding per NFPA 70 (National Electrical Code) Article 250, including a grounding electrode conductor and equipment bonding.

## Step 7: Maintenance Approach

How you maintain the generator is as important as what you buy. The two primary approaches are service contracts with the generator dealer or distributor, and in-house maintenance performed by facility staff.

### Maintenance Approach Comparison

| Factor | Service Contract | In-House Maintenance |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Cost structure** | Fixed annual fee; predictable budgeting | Parts and labor as needed; variable |
| **Typical annual cost (500 kW diesel)** | $5,000-$12,000 | $2,000-$6,000 (parts + staff time) |
| **Expertise** | Factory-trained technicians with OEM parts | Depends on staff training and experience |
| **Response time** | Defined in contract (typically 4-24 hours) | Immediate for trained staff; varies |
| **Parts availability** | Dealer maintains inventory | Must stock critical spares or wait for delivery |
| **Documentation** | Provided by service provider | Facility must maintain own records |
| **Liability** | Service provider shares maintenance liability | Full liability remains with facility |
| **Load bank testing** | Typically included or available as add-on | Must rent or purchase load bank equipment |
| **Best for** | Facilities without dedicated mechanical staff | Facilities with trained engineers on staff |

Many facilities use a hybrid approach: in-house staff perform weekly and monthly inspections and exercise tests, while a service contractor handles semi-annual and annual maintenance that requires specialized tools and expertise.

<Callout type="info" title="Contract Essentials">
If you choose a service contract, ensure it explicitly covers: all fluids and filters, annual load bank testing, ATS inspection, battery testing and replacement, 24/7 emergency response, and detailed maintenance documentation provided to you after each service visit. Get the response time commitment in writing with defined penalties for missed SLAs.
</Callout>

## Step 8: Total Cost of Ownership Analysis

The purchase price of the generator is typically 40-55% of the total first-year cost and a smaller fraction of the lifetime cost. A complete total cost of ownership (TCO) analysis should include all of the following elements.

### TCO Framework

| Cost Category | Typical Range (500 kW Diesel) | Notes |
|--------------|------------------------------|-------|
| Generator package | $150,000-$300,000 | Engine, alternator, controller, enclosure |
| ATS | $15,000-$40,000 | Depends on amperage and features |
| Installation (mechanical) | $30,000-$75,000 | Pad, fuel system, exhaust, ventilation |
| Installation (electrical) | $40,000-$100,000 | Wiring, conduit, grounding, integration |
| Permitting and engineering | $10,000-$30,000 | Design, permits, inspections, commissioning |
| **Total installed cost** | **$245,000-$545,000** | |
| Annual fuel (testing only) | $1,500-$3,000 | 25-50 hours of operation per year |
| Annual maintenance | $3,000-$12,000 | Depends on contract vs. in-house |
| Major overhaul (every 10-15 years) | $30,000-$75,000 | Top end, injectors, turbo, alternator bearings |
| **20-year lifecycle cost** | **$350,000-$850,000** | Highly dependent on outage frequency |

When comparing proposals, insist that vendors provide installed pricing, not just equipment pricing. The lowest equipment price often comes with the highest installation cost due to non-standard configurations or missing accessories.

## Questions to Ask Vendors Before Purchasing

Before signing a purchase order, get clear answers to each of these questions:

1. **What is the total installed price**, including pad, fuel system, ATS, wiring, permitting, and commissioning?
2. **What is the lead time** from order to operational commissioning?
3. **What warranty is included**, and what does it cover? What are the warranty conditions (e.g., required maintenance by authorized dealer)?
4. **Where is the nearest authorized service center**, and what is the guaranteed emergency response time?
5. **What parts are stocked locally** versus ordered from regional or national distribution?
6. **What is the expected fuel consumption** at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% load?
7. **What is the noise level** at the specified measurement distance, and what enclosure options are available?
8. **Does the generator meet current EPA Tier 4 Final standards**, and can you provide the emissions certification documentation?
9. **What remote monitoring capability** is included or available, and what are the ongoing subscription costs?
10. **What are the maintenance intervals and estimated annual maintenance costs** for the first five years?
11. **Can you provide references** from facilities of similar size and type in this geographic area?
12. **What training** is provided to facility staff upon commissioning?

## Making the Final Decision

The right generator for your facility is the one that reliably powers your critical loads, meets all applicable codes and regulations, fits your site constraints, and delivers the lowest total cost of ownership over its service life.

Resist the temptation to make the decision solely on equipment price. A generator that costs $20,000 less but requires more expensive maintenance, has slower emergency response from the nearest dealer, or needs a noise enclosure upgrade after installation is not actually cheaper.

Invest the time in a proper load analysis, compare total installed costs, verify local service infrastructure, and confirm code compliance with your AHJ before the equipment arrives. The decisions you make during the purchasing process determine the compliance posture and operational reliability of your emergency power system for the next two decades.
